Embarrassing Couple Quiz: How Far Do You Dare to Go?

Kissing in public, ordering for your partner, calling them "snuggle bear pumpkin" in front of their colleagues... Every couple has their slightly embarrassing habits. The question is: how many do you have, and do you own them?

Two ways to play, your pick. The test: fifteen real-life situations and a verdict on how much cringe you own up to. The game: twenty awkward questions to ask each other out loud, no safety net.

Ready for the test?

All 15 questions, in order. Start the test above to get your result.

  1. You're at a friend's party. How do you handle kissing in front of people?

    • We don't overthink it. If we feel like making out, we go for it.
    • It can last a while if the mood's right. People can deal.
    • A quick peck here and there, but we keep it classy.
    • Never in public. That stays at home.

  2. At a restaurant, your partner can't decide what to order. What do you do?

    • We order for them and negotiate the details with the waiter.
    • We pick something for them without asking. We know their taste.
    • We suggest a couple of dishes to help them choose.
    • We wait patiently for them to decide on their own.

  3. On public transport, your partner falls asleep on your shoulder. How do you react?

    • We wake them up with a real kiss, no matter who's watching.
    • We give them little kisses on the neck. Passengers can look.
    • We stroke their hand or kiss their forehead, discreetly.
    • We freeze so we don't wake them up.

  4. Someone overhears the pet name you use for your partner. How embarrassing is it?

    • It's objectively cringe, like "snookums" or "baby boo boo", and we own it.
    • It's cute and slips out naturally. No shame.
    • It might slip out, but it's a pretty tame one.
    • We never use pet names in front of people. That's private.

  5. You're at your partner's parents' place and an argument starts. What happens?

    • We hash it out right there. The parents are family, they can handle it.
    • We don't yell but the tension is obvious. The parents have noticed.
    • We go to a quiet corner and talk it out in whispers.
    • We put on a smile and save it for when we're alone.

  6. Your partner says something wrong at a group dinner. What do you do?

    • We correct them in front of everyone and explain exactly why they're wrong.
    • We correct them directly, trying to be nice about it.
    • We slip in a subtle correction so they don't keep going.
    • We let it go and bring it up later in private.

  7. You're stuck in a long queue together. What do you do?

    • We start tickle fights, make dares, or pull silly faces.
    • We cuddle and do our little couple things. People get it.
    • We chat and quietly comment on the people around us.
    • We each scroll through our phones.

  8. Your partner farts in public. How do you react?

    • We rate it, comment on it, and compare it to last time. Out loud.
    • We burst out laughing, no hiding it.
    • We share a knowing look but keep our composure.
    • We pretend it didn't happen.

  9. At the grocery store, your partner sneaks something unplanned into the cart. What happens?

    • Full-on debate in the aisle, "you said we were being careful!", then we buy it anyway.
    • We put it back and explain why. Mini negotiation on the spot.
    • We raise an eyebrow, they explain, and that's that.
    • We say nothing. Everyone deserves a little treat.

  10. At a party, your partner's dancing but you're not. What do you do?

    • We create a full moment, impromptu tango, recruit a dance group, the whole show.
    • We end up dancing too. Badly. Don't care.
    • We move closer, put our hands on their hips, and sway along.
    • We watch from a distance with a smile. We don't move.

  11. Your partner has something stuck in their teeth. What do you do?

    • We try to get it out ourselves, right there. People can watch.
    • We tell them straight up, in front of everyone. It's for their own good.
    • We signal with our eyes and a quick hand gesture.
    • We lean in and whisper it in their ear.

  12. You're taking a couple's photo in a public place. What's the vibe?

    • Full photo shoot: retakes, poses, maybe a stranger recruited as photographer.
    • We pose, redo it, direct each other until it's perfect.
    • A few quick poses, just finding the right angle.
    • One natural photo, no staging, done.

  13. At the cinema, your partner starts crying during the film. How do you react?

    • We cry too and hold each other tight, forget the neighbours.
    • We give them a proper hug, no worries about the people next to us.
    • We take their hand and lean in close.
    • We quietly pass them a tissue.

  14. You're arguing in the street. What happens?

    • We let it all out right there. Volume? Not guaranteed.
    • We stop walking and say what needs to be said. Passers-by take a detour.
    • We keep walking and talk it through in a low voice.
    • We go silent and save it for home.

  15. You drop by your partner's office (open plan, colleagues around). How does it go?

    • We walk in like we own the place, kiss, settle in, comment on the desk decor.
    • Kiss, cuddle, a little moment. The colleagues know we're together.
    • Quick kiss and a hello to the nearby colleagues.
    • We wave from the door and wait for them to come over.

What exactly is an "embarrassing" couple?

Embarrassment in a couple is subjective. For some people, two people cuddling on the subway is already too much. For others, ordering for your partner at a restaurant is cute. What everyone calls an "embarrassing couple" is actually a couple that steps outside their private zone and lets things spill into public spaces that would conventionally stay within the walls of their home.

It's not a judgement. It's an observation.

Very close-knit couples often fit this profile. Not because they lack tact, but because their bubble is so dense that it takes up a bit more space than expected. They've developed their codes, their rituals, their way of being together, and sometimes they export them. The people around see something that doesn't belong to them, and that's what creates the feeling of embarrassment.

Is it a big deal to be an embarrassing couple?

No. Not really. There are obviously limits: behaviours that genuinely make people uncomfortable in spaces where they can't leave, situations where the public display becomes a form of disrespect towards the context. A funeral is not a party with friends. A professional open-plan office is not a flat.

But in the vast majority of cases, an "embarrassing" couple is just a couple that likes being together, that doesn't overthink what people think, and that has a way of interacting that slightly exceeds social conventions. Most of the time, the people around find it endearing far more often than they'll admit.

What really becomes a problem is when one of the two is much more comfortable than the other with this public display. When one finds it natural and the other manages it by smiling without really feeling good about it. That asymmetry deserves a real conversation, not to put an end to the impulses, but to make sure both people are in the same place.

Why are some couples more demonstrative than others?

It's a combination of factors, and none of them are really deliberate. Family history plays a big role. People who grew up in families where affection is expressed physically and openly find it natural to reproduce that in their adult relationships. Those who had a more reserved upbringing often feel a real inner resistance to showing anything in public, even when they want to.

The length of the relationship also changes things. At the beginning, couples tend to be either very demonstrative (the "we can't help it" phase) or very discreet (we don't know where we stand yet). Over time, a kind of balance settles in. Couples who remain very demonstrative after several years have generally chosen this way of functioning consciously, even if it's not always said explicitly.

And then there's simply personality. Some people constitutionally don't care about other people's gaze. Not out of bravado, not out of exhibitionism, just because that particular antenna is less developed in them. They live their relationships with the same intensity in private and in public, without really making the distinction.

What if you would rather ask the awkward questions?

Searching for an “awkward couple quiz” means two very different things depending on who is asking. Some want to know whether they are the couple that makes out in front of the parents. Others are looking for awkward questions to ask each other, the ones nobody brings up on a Tuesday night in front of a series.

That is what the second format is for: twenty questions drawn from four families. No filter, for everything about bodies and the daily life nobody else sees. Before us, for exes and first times, ground the purity test puts a number on. Your family, my friends, for everything you think about the other's people and never say. And the truth that stings, where you finally ask what you never asked.

There is no score and no winner. You read the question out loud, you answer one after the other, and you get to skip once per round. It is a two-player game on a single phone, better at night, better when you are ready to hear the answer.

How to tell if your couple is in the "fun embarrassing" or "problematic embarrassing" zone?

The line is blurry, but it exists. A few useful markers. If the people around you are smiling (even while looking away), you're in the "fun embarrassing" zone. If the people around you are crossing the street or staring at their shoes with unusual intensity, you're approaching the line.

If you and your partner laugh about the same situations afterwards, that's a good sign. If one of you regularly has to "manage" the social fallout of what the other did, there's an imbalance worth looking at.

If your friends still invite you over, it means you haven't crossed the tolerance threshold. That's a concrete and underestimated indicator.

And if you took this quiz together and got very different scores, take five minutes to compare your answers question by question. The gaps are often more interesting than the final score.
