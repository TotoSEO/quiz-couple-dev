Test: are you ready for a baby?

20 questions to assess your readiness for parenthood: emotional maturity, finances, relationship, lifestyle. An honest and kind stocktake.

  1. When you picture your life with a baby, your first reaction is…

    • A mix of excitement and calm, you feel ready to welcome that change.
    • Curiosity and desire, even if a few uncertainties remain.
    • Slight apprehension, you like the idea but it still feels abstract.
    • Anxiety or a mental block, you can't picture yourself in that role.

  2. Financially, how would you rate your current situation for welcoming a child?

    • You have solid savings, a stable income and you've already worked out what a baby costs.
    • Your situation is decent, you could adapt by reorganising some spending.
    • It's a bit tight, you'd need to shore up your finances before seriously considering it.
    • You're in a precarious financial situation and a child would add considerable stress.

  3. How would you describe your current relationship?

    • Solid, stable and fulfilling, you communicate well and get through hard times together.
    • Broadly good, with a few things to improve but nothing worrying.
    • Still being built or fragile on some points, communication could be better.
    • Unstable or non-existent, this isn't the right context to consider a baby.

  4. Are you ready to give up a large part of your sleep for several months?

    • Yes, you know it will be hard and you feel able to handle that tiredness.
    • You know it'll be tough but you think you can organise yourself to cope.
    • The idea of broken nights scares you a little, sleep matters a lot to you.
    • Not at all, you already struggle to function on little sleep.

  5. Do you have a support network (family, close friends) you could count on?

    • Yes, you have people nearby who are available and ready to help in practical ways.
    • You have a few trusted people, even if they're not all close by.
    • Your network is limited, you'd mostly have to rely on yourself.
    • You feel fairly isolated, there's no one you can really lean on.

  6. How do you see a baby affecting your career?

    • You have a clear plan: parental leave, return to work, childcare sorted.
    • You've thought about it without planning everything, but you know you'll find solutions.
    • It worries you, your work situation is still fragile or in transition.
    • You haven't thought about it at all, or your career is your absolute priority right now.

  7. How have you felt about your mental health these past few months?

    • Stable and settled, you handle your stress and emotions well day to day.
    • Pretty good, with normal ups and downs you know how to manage.
    • Fragile at times, you're going through a somewhat complicated period.
    • Difficult, you're dealing with anxiety, depression or significant burnout.

  8. Are you and your partner aligned on wanting a child?

    • Yes, you've talked about it openly and the desire is shared.
    • Broadly, even if the timing or some details still need discussing.
    • Not really, one of you is more enthusiastic than the other.
    • No, there's a clear disagreement or the subject has never come up.

  9. What's your housing situation?

    • You have suitable space, with a room available for a child.
    • Your current home could work for a while, even if moving would be ideal.
    • Your home is small or poorly suited, a change would be needed before a baby arrives.
    • Your housing situation is unstable or very precarious.

  10. Can you stay patient in stressful everyday situations?

    • Yes, you're fairly calm and patient, even under pressure.
    • Most of the time, though you have your limits like everyone.
    • You lose your temper quite easily and you know patience isn't your strong suit.
    • You often feel overwhelmed by stress, even over minor things.

  11. How do you react to a baby or young child crying around you?

    • You stay calm and naturally try to work out what's wrong.
    • It affects you, you do your best to help even when it isn't easy.
    • It makes you uncomfortable or stresses you out quickly.
    • It's completely beyond you, you'd rather move away.

  12. Are you ready to put some hobbies or passions on hold for the first few years?

    • Yes, you know it's temporary and you accept it calmly.
    • You're ready to adjust your pace, even if it takes some effort.
    • That sacrifice weighs on you just thinking about it.
    • No, your personal activities are essential to your balance and you don't want to give them up.

  13. Have you thought about the mental load and how parenting tasks would be shared?

    • Yes, you've discussed it as a couple and you have a clear view of the split.
    • You've thought about it and you know it matters, but nothing is settled yet.
    • Vaguely, you assume it'll sort itself out along the way.
    • Not at all, you have no idea what it involves in practice.

  14. How do you handle unexpected events and changes of plan in your life?

    • You adapt easily, surprises don't throw you.
    • You cope fine, though you prefer things planned.
    • Unexpected events stress you a lot and disrupt your daily life.
    • You need to control everything, the slightest change puts you in a difficult state.

  15. Do you feel emotionally mature enough to raise a child?

    • Yes, you've done real work on yourself and you know yourself well.
    • You think so, even if you know there are still things to work on.
    • You're not sure, you're still figuring yourself out in some areas.
    • No, you feel you still have a long way to go before being ready.

  16. What's your main motivation for having a child?

    • A deep, considered desire, driven by the wish to pass things on and accompany a new life.
    • The desire is growing naturally, you feel you're in a good phase of life for it.
    • Social or family pressure plays a part, even if there's some genuine desire.
    • You don't have a clear motivation, or you hope a baby would fill a void.

  17. Have you ever looked after a baby or young child for an extended period?

    • Yes, regularly, and it confirmed your desire.
    • A few times, and you did pretty well.
    • Very rarely, you don't have much experience with very young children.
    • Never, you have no idea what daily life with a baby looks like.

  18. How do you feel about your own childhood and the upbringing you received?

    • You've made peace with your past and you know what kind of parent you want to be, regardless of your models.
    • You've gained perspective, you want to repeat some things and avoid others.
    • It's a sensitive subject, some wounds haven't healed yet.
    • Your childhood was hard and you're afraid of repeating the same patterns.

  19. Are you ready to ask for help if you need it (doctor, therapist, people around you)?

    • Absolutely, you know asking for help is a strength, not a weakness.
    • You think so, even if it isn't always easy for you.
    • You'd struggle, you generally prefer to manage on your own.
    • No, asking for help feels impossible or shameful to you.

  20. If you had to sum up how you feel about having a baby now…

    • You feel confident and enthusiastic, the conditions are there to go for it.
    • You're fairly positive, a few details remain but the essentials are in place.
    • You're hesitating, some important things aren't in place yet.
    • You feel it isn't the right time, and it's okay to acknowledge that.

The real signs of emotional maturity for becoming a parent

Emotional maturity isn't the absence of fear. It's the ability to recognise your fears, name them and move forward anyway. A future parent who is emotionally ready knows how to handle their own emotions before having to handle a newborn's at 3 in the morning. That comes down to several concrete things: being able to ask for help without shame, accepting that you can't control everything, and having made a minimum of peace with your own family history. Research in perinatal psychology shows that parents who have worked on their attachment style before a child arrives experience a calmer transition into parenthood. It isn't a question of perfection, it's a question of self-awareness. If you're able to step back from your reactions, apologise when you're wrong and tolerate emotional discomfort, you already have a solid base.

Financial readiness: what does a baby really cost?

Let's talk numbers, because love doesn't pay for nappies. In most Western countries, a baby costs on average the equivalent of 500 to 800 euros a month in the first year: nappies, feeding, clothes, equipment, health costs not covered by insurance. And that's before childcare, which can easily double the bill. Being financially ready doesn't mean being wealthy. It means having a clear view of your income, your spending and your ability to absorb an additional cost without financial stress becoming permanent. Having a small savings cushion, knowing your entitlements (parental leave, family allowances, birth grants where they exist) and having thought through childcare are markers of realistic preparation. If your finances are fragile, that isn't a ban, it's a signal to shore things up before taking the plunge.

A solid relationship: an indispensable foundation

A baby doesn't save a struggling relationship. That's a dangerous myth the statistics clearly contradict: according to a study in the Journal of Family Psychology, roughly 67% of couples see a drop in relationship satisfaction in the first three years after a birth. The couples who come through it best are those who already had solid communication, healthy conflict management and well-anchored mutual respect. Before considering a baby, ask yourself honestly: is your relationship in a good place? Can you argue without destroying each other? Does the wish for a child come from both of you, or mainly from one partner? Before a decision of that size, it is worth knowing what already holds and what doesn't: they are the same markers that make a relationship healthy.

The lifestyle adjustments to anticipate

A baby doesn't slot into your current life, it transforms it. Lie-ins, spontaneous outings, quiet evenings, travelling light: all of that will change, at least for a while. This isn't meant to frighten you, it's meant to prepare you. The parents who handle the transition best are those who had realistic expectations. They knew the first six months would be a marathon of tiredness and adaptation. They had accepted the idea of putting some hobbies on hold. And above all, they didn't see those sacrifices as losses, but as conscious choices. In practical terms, think about your current daily rhythm. Is it compatible with a newborn's needs? If you live at full speed between work, sport and a social life, you'll need an adjustment period. And if you're thinking of moving in together, settle that question before the baby arrives, not at the same time.

Career and parenthood: finding the right balance

The work question is often the one people put off the longest, and yet it weighs heavily on the decision. Maternity leave, paternity leave, returning to work, childcare, a possible reduction in hours: every choice has concrete implications for your daily life and your finances. Statutory leave varies a great deal from one country to another, and in every case it's a start, not a complete solution. The real question is what happens afterwards. Who goes back to work first? Who reduces their hours if needed? How do you handle the mental load of organising childcare? These conversations deserve to happen before the pregnancy, not during. Because the mental load, if it isn't anticipated and shared, becomes one of the first sources of tension in young families.

Why a support network matters so much for new parents

You make a baby with two people, but you raise one with a village. That African proverb has never been more relevant. Studies show that social support is one of the strongest protective factors against parental burnout and postnatal depression. Having grandparents available, friends who've been through it, a kind neighbour: all of that counts enormously. If the people close to you are far away or not very available, that isn't a deal-breaker, but it needs compensating for. Identify the resources around you: new parent groups, breastfeeding support networks, local health services, online forums. The key is not to be left alone with a newborn and the feeling that you should be handling everything. Happy parenting runs on support, not on individual performance.

Getting ready mentally: genuine desire versus social pressure

There's a fundamental difference between wanting a child and feeling obliged to want one. Social pressure, family pressure, the famous "so, when are you two having kids?", friends having one baby after another: all of it can blur the line between authentic desire and conformity. Before moving forward, take the time to ask yourself, alone with yourself: if nobody asked me the question, if nobody expected it, would I still want a child? The answer doesn't have to be an enthusiastic yes. It can be a "yes, but not now" or an "I need time to think about it". All of those answers are valid. What isn't valid is having a baby by default, under pressure or out of fear of missing the boat. A child deserves to arrive in a family that chose them, not in a family that gave in.

The right moment vs the perfect moment: why perfection is a trap

If you wait for everything to line up perfectly before having a baby, you never will. The ideal home, the ideal salary, ideal maturity, the ideal relationship: that scenario only exists in films. In reality, the right moment is recognisable by a few concrete signals: your relationship is stable, your finances are sufficient (not necessarily comfortable), your desire is sincere and shared, and you feel able to face the unknown. That's it. The rest you'll learn along the way, like every parent before you. This parenthood test for couples can round out your thinking if you want to assess your readiness together. Because parenting is a team project, and the best teams are the ones that prepare for the match together, not the ones that improvise on the day.

FAQ

How do I know if I'm ready for a baby?
There's no universal checklist, but several indicators can guide you: sufficient emotional and financial stability, a sincere desire (not just one driven by social pressure), a solid relationship, an identified support network and the capacity to accept that your life is going to change profoundly. This test helps you assess these different aspects concretely.
Is it normal to be afraid of having a baby?
Absolutely. Fear is in fact a sign of clear-sightedness: it shows you grasp the scale of what's ahead. A total absence of fear would be more worrying. What matters is that the fear isn't paralysing and that you can talk about it, with your partner, someone close or a professional. Expectant parents who dare to name their fears generally experience a smoother transition into parenthood.
What's the right age to have a first child?
There's no universally ideal age. Medically, female fertility begins to decline after 35, but emotional maturity, financial stability and the quality of the relationship count just as much. Some couples at 25 are more ready than others at 38. The best indicator remains a sincere and shared desire, combined with a realistic capacity to welcome a child into your life.
My partner doesn't want children, what should I do?
This is one of the most delicate disagreements in a relationship. The first step is to understand the nature of that refusal: is it a "not now" or a "never"? Open the conversation without pressure or ultimatums. If the subject stays blocked, a couples therapist can help you explore that gap in a neutral setting. Forcing or manipulating the other person is never an option, a child has to be wanted by both parents.
Can a baby strengthen a fragile relationship?
No, and it's often the opposite. A baby amplifies the dynamics already present in a relationship: if communication is good, it can strengthen around the shared project. But if tensions exist, lack of sleep, stress and the mental load will make them worse. Studies show that the couples who hold up best after a birth are those who already had solid foundations. Before thinking about a baby, make sure your relationship is in good health.
