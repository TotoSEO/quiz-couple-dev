Online Test | Are You Ready to Have Children? readiness

You've been thinking about it for a few weeks. Maybe a few months. The idea of a child keeps coming back in your conversations, and in your silences too. And then one day, the question hits for real: are we actually ready?

This parenthood readiness test won't give you a yes-or-no answer (nobody can). But it will force both of you to face things honestly. 20 straight-up questions about your relationship, your finances, your lifestyle, your fears. Answer separately, then compare. It's often in the gap between your answers that the real conversations are hiding.

Questions preview

Here is a preview of the questions covered. Start the quiz above to get your personalized result.

  1. Why do you want a child?

    • Because we both deeply want one, we've been talking about it for a long time and it's a well-thought-out decision.
    • The desire is there, but it's mostly one of us pushing while the other goes along.
    • Our parents are pressuring us, all our friends have kids, so we figure it's time.
    • We're hoping it will bring us closer or give our relationship new meaning.

  2. Are you ready to wake up every night for 2 to 3 years?

    • We know it's going to be tough. We've talked about it and even planned how to take turns.
    • We expect the nights will be rough, but we'll figure it out as we go.
    • Meh, babies eventually learn to sleep, right? It can't last that long.

  3. Could your current financial situation handle an extra $500 to $800 in monthly expenses?

    • Yes, we have savings and our income comfortably covers that extra cost.
    • It would be tight, but doable with some adjustments.
    • Honestly, we're already struggling to make ends meet.

  4. How do you handle conflicts in your relationship today?

    • We talk it out, even when it's tense. We always end up finding common ground.
    • It depends on the topic. On some things, we get stuck and let it drag on.
    • We either fight or give each other the silent treatment. Then it blows over.

  5. Have you discussed your parenting values together?

    • Yes, in depth. We know where we agree and where we'll need to compromise.
    • Vaguely. We've touched on the topic without going into detail.
    • Not really, we'll figure it out when the time comes.

  6. Who will take care of the baby day-to-day? Have you discussed this concretely?

    • We have a clear plan: parental leave, shift arrangements, nighttime and task responsibilities.
    • We know it'll be a shared effort, but we haven't worked out the details yet.
    • We haven't discussed it. It'll work itself out naturally.

  7. How do you react to extreme stress and exhaustion?

    • I get irritable, but I'm aware of it and I adjust.
    • I withdraw into myself. It's not great, but I'm conscious of it.
    • I lose it. Exhaustion is really not my thing.

  8. Is your relationship going through a stable period right now?

    • Yes, we're in a good place. Solid.
    • Some tension, but nothing serious. Just a minor rough patch.
    • It's complicated. We're not even sure it's going to last.

  9. Are you ready to put your social life on hold for at least 1 to 2 years?

    • Yes, I've already made peace with saying goodbye to spontaneous nights out and lazy mornings.
    • I know things will change, but I hope to keep at least some social life.
    • Uh... no. I'm not ready to give that up.

  10. How do you currently split household chores?

    • It's fairly balanced, each of us does their share without being asked.
    • One of us does more than the other, but we talk about it.
    • It's unbalanced and it's a recurring source of tension.

  11. Do you have suitable housing (or housing that could be adapted) for a child?

    • Yes, we have enough space and stability.
    • Not ideal, but we have a plan to move or make adjustments.
    • We're in a tiny apartment with no prospect of change anytime soon.

  12. Your relationship with your own parents: what impact does it have on your desire for a child?

    • I've made peace with my childhood (good or difficult). I know what kind of parent I want to be.
    • There are unresolved things, but I'm working on them.
    • I'm afraid of repeating my parents' mistakes. It holds me back.

  13. Is your professional situation compatible with having a child?

    • Yes: permanent job or stable income, leave available, understanding employer.
    • It's manageable, but we'd have to juggle a bit.
    • It's a total blur career-wise. Having a child now would be a leap into the unknown.

  14. Do you have a support network around you (family, close friends)?

    • Yes, reliable and available people who can help us.
    • A few people, but not necessarily nearby.
    • No, we'd be truly on our own in this.

  15. If you found out tomorrow that you're expecting a child, what would your honest first reaction be?

    • Immense joy, even if a little scary.
    • Surprise, a mix of excitement and anxiety.
    • Panic. We're not ready at all.

  16. Do you agree on the number of children you want?

    • Yes, we've discussed it and we're aligned.
    • Not exactly, but we're open to discussion.
    • We've never talked about it, or we completely disagree.

  17. How do you feel around other people's children?

    • I love it. Even when it's noisy and chaotic, it makes me smile.
    • It depends. An hour is fine, a whole day is exhausting.
    • I can't stand the crying, screaming, and mess. It stresses me out deeply.

  18. Do you have important personal projects that would be incompatible with a baby?

    • No, or they're projects we can adapt. The timing is right.
    • Yes, but I'm ready to postpone or slow them down.
    • Yes, and giving them up now would make me unhappy.

  19. Are you aware that your relationship will be put to the test?

    • Yes. We know the first months will be intense. We've talked about how to support each other.
    • We suspect so, but we don't really have a plan.
    • A child brings you closer together. We don't see the problem.

  20. Being 100% honest with yourself: do you truly feel ready to become a parent?

    • Yes. Not perfectly ready (nobody is), but ready to try, to learn, to commit.
    • I'm still hesitating. The desire is there, but something holds me back.
    • No. And deep down, I think I know it.

Questions to ask before having a child: why it's so important to talk about it

We all know a couple who had a baby "to see what happens." Spoiler: it rarely goes as planned. And it makes sense. A child doesn't put your relationship on pause, it hits fast-forward. What was going well gets better, what was slightly irritating becomes an open wound.

The questions to ask before having children aren't there to cool down your desire. It's the opposite. They're there to make sure your desire is grounded in reality, not in an Instagram fantasy of pastel onesies and rattan cribs. The reality of a newborn is 3 hours of sleep a night, diaper changes at 2 AM, and a libido that goes on vacation (a long one).

According to a study published in the Journal of Family Psychology, about 67% of couples experience a decline in relationship satisfaction within the first 3 years after a child's birth. That number isn't there to scare you. It's there to say one simple thing: the couples who do best are those who anticipated the difficulties. Not the ones with the most money or the biggest apartment. The ones who talked. Really talked.

The 5 topics every couple should discuss before having a baby

Money

A child costs on average between $500 and $800 per month in the first year. Diapers, formula, childcare, clothes they outgrow in 3 weeks... Having a financial cushion, even a modest one, changes everything. Financial strain is one of the leading causes of tension in young families.

Parenting duties

This is THE topic that makes or breaks couples after a baby arrives. Who gets up at night? Who handles doctor's appointments? Studies show women still take on 65-70% of domestic and childcare duties. Talking about this before the baby arrives is non-negotiable.

Parenting style

Strict parents vs laid-back family where everything was negotiable? These differences become fault lines when you have to decide whether to set firm boundaries or give more freedom. Talk about it now.

Relationship impact

Love alone isn't enough to absorb the shock of the first months. You also need organization, patience, and the ability to see each other at your worst without it destroying everything.

Fears

Fear of not being good enough. Fear of repeating your parents' mistakes. Fear of losing your freedom, your identity. These fears are normal. What's not normal is ignoring them. A fear expressed loses half its power.

Try this: each of you writes down your 3 biggest concerns about parenthood, then compare them. The gaps often reveal the most important conversations to have.

How to know if you're ready for a child: the real signs

Spoiler: the "perfect moment" doesn't exist. If you wait until everything is aligned (finances, career, housing, emotional maturity, waxing moon, and Mercury in retrograde), you'll never get there. But there are signs that don't lie.

You're probably ready if your relationship is in a good place, if you can handle conflicts without destroying each other, if the desire comes from both of you (and not from social pressure), and if you've at least discussed the topics mentioned above. Conversely, if your relationship is shaky, if you're hoping a baby will fix everything, or if one of you isn't really on board... wait.

A parenthood readiness test like the one you just took doesn't replace real introspection (and certainly not professional guidance if needed). But it's a good starting point to open the dialogue.

Being ready doesn't mean having no fear. It means trusting your relationship enough to move forward despite the fear.

The mistake 90% of couples make: not talking about the "after"

People talk a lot about "should we have a baby?", about the pregnancy, the delivery, the name. And then the baby arrives. And it's a black hole. Nobody predicted that daily life would look like this.

The "after" is the first 6 months where you'll operate in survival mode. It's the mother feeling alone even though the father is there. It's the father not knowing what to do because nobody taught him. It's the arguments at 3 AM because the baby is crying and everyone is at their breaking point.

We're not saying this to be dramatic. We're saying it because couples who talk about this beforehand get through this period more smoothly. Ask yourselves: who does what during maternity leave? Will the co-parent take leave too? How do we handle family visits? Do we allow ourselves to ask for help? All these questions to ask as a couple before having a child seem trivial on paper. In practice, they make an enormous difference.

If your relationship is solid, these challenges will be trials you weather together. If not, now is the time to strengthen your foundation before adding a baby to the equation.

Practical exercise: spend a full day with a baby (nephew, niece, friend's child). Not to play perfect family, but to observe how you handle exhaustion, the unexpected, and dividing tasks together.

The timing question: is there an ideal age?

Medically, female fertility begins to decline significantly after age 35. That's a biological fact, not a judgment. On the male side, sperm quality also decreases with age, even though it's discussed less often.

But timing isn't just about biology. It's also about your emotional stability, your relational maturity, your material circumstances. Some couples at 25 are more ready than others at 38. Being a young parent means having physical energy but sometimes less perspective. Being an older parent is the opposite. There's no magic formula.

What we know for certain is that couples who have a child because they truly want to (and not "before it's too late," not "because everyone else is") navigate the transition to parenthood better. A sincere, shared desire is the best sign of all.

Retaking the test in 6 months: why it's a good idea

Your score today is a snapshot of a specific moment. Your answers will evolve. Your finances may change, your career situation too. Your relationship will face new challenges (or conversely, grow stronger). That's why we recommend retaking this parenthood readiness test in a few months.

The point isn't to wait for a perfect score. It's to see if your answers are moving in the right direction. If the topics that were blocking you are getting unblocked. And above all, if the desire is growing or fading, because both are valid.

In the meantime, parenthood is also a question of day-to-day compatibility. Knowing whether you have enough in common to build a project this big matters. And if marriage is also part of the conversation, it's worth checking that you're aligned on that question too.

Frequently asked questions about parenthood and relationships

Is it normal to be afraid of having a child?
Yes, absolutely. Being afraid doesn't mean you're not ready. It means you appreciate the magnitude of what's ahead, and that's actually healthy. The real problem isn't being afraid. It's not being able to talk about it with your partner.
My partner doesn't want children and I do, what should we do?
This is one of the most painful disagreements in a relationship, because there's no obvious compromise. The first step is understanding why. Is it a 'not now' or a 'never'? Talk about it without pressure, ideally with the help of a couples therapist.
Can a baby save a relationship in crisis?
No. That's a dangerous myth. A baby amplifies existing dynamics. If your relationship is going well, a child will probably strengthen your bond. If your relationship is struggling, a baby will add stress. Before thinking about a baby, check whether your relationship has toxic traits.
At what age are you too old to have a child?
There's no universal answer. Reproductive medicine has pushed biological limits (IVF, egg freezing...), but later pregnancies statistically carry more risks. What matters is making this decision with your doctor and your partner.
How do I bring up the topic of children with my partner?
Avoid the dramatic 'we need to talk' on a Sunday evening. Bring it up naturally, in a calm moment. You can even use this test as a conversation starter: 'Hey, I found this parenthood readiness test online, want to take it together?'
How much does a child cost in the first year?
On average, expect to spend between $6,000 and $12,000 in the first year, not including childcare (which can easily double the bill). The biggest expenses: diapers (around $600-$900/year), food, clothing, gear, and out-of-pocket medical costs.
