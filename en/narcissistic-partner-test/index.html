Are you in a relationship with a narcissist?

A confidential, caring self-assessment to help you put words on what you're going through.

Ready for the test?

All 20 questions, in order. Start the test above to get your result.

  1. At the very start of your relationship, how would you describe their behaviour?

    • They got to know me gradually, at my own pace, without rushing.
    • Attentive, with a few intense bursts that felt sincere.
    • Everything moved very fast: 'soulmate' talk, passionate declarations, immediate plans.
    • They showered me with attention… then changed abruptly once I was attached.

  2. When they make a hurtful remark about you in front of other people…

    • It doesn't happen: they're careful never to humiliate me in public.
    • It's rare, and they apologise sincerely afterwards.
    • They throw digs 'as a joke' and say I have no sense of humour if I'm upset.
    • They belittle me often, then make me look oversensitive or dramatic.

  3. When you point out something they said or did, their typical reaction is…

    • They listen, own their part, and we talk it through calmly.
    • They get defensive at first, but end up admitting the facts.
    • They flatly deny it: 'I never said that, you're making it up.'
    • They make me doubt my memory and my sanity ('you're paranoid', 'you're imagining things').

  4. Since you've been together, your relationships with your loved ones (friends, family)…

    • Have grown richer: they get along well with the people close to me.
    • Haven't really changed.
    • Have thinned out: they often criticise my friends or family.
    • Have shrunk a lot: I see my loved ones far less to avoid tension.

  5. When you're sad or going through a hard time, they…

    • Support me and genuinely try to comfort me.
    • Aren't always comfortable with it, but make an effort to be there.
    • Minimise what I feel or quickly turn the conversation back to themselves.
    • Stay cold, even annoyed, as if my pain were an inconvenience.

  6. After a conflict, who ends up apologising?

    • Whoever was wrong, and often both of us.
    • Often me, but they acknowledge their mistakes too.
    • Almost always me, even when I did nothing wrong.
    • Always me: they cast themselves as the victim, never as responsible.

  7. When it comes to your personal choices (spending, clothes, going out, free time)…

    • I decide freely; we talk about the things that concern us both.
    • They give their opinion, but respect my choices.
    • They apply pressure: I self-censor to avoid reproaches.
    • They control my money, my clothes or my schedule.

  8. The partner your friends know and the one you live with at home…

    • Are the same person, consistent in every situation.
    • Are quite alike, with small, normal differences.
    • Differ: they're far more charming in public than with me.
    • Are two opposite people: lovely outside, contemptuous or harsh in private.

  9. When they're displeased with you, the way they show it is…

    • To talk about it openly to resolve the problem.
    • To sulk for a while, then come back to me.
    • To give me the cold shoulder and punish me with silence for hours.
    • To ignore me completely for days until I give in and apologise.

  10. Day to day, at home, you feel mostly…

    • Relaxed and free to be myself.
    • At ease, with the normal tensions of living together.
    • On guard: I anticipate their moods to avoid outbursts.
    • In a constant state of alert: I feel like I'm 'walking on eggshells'.

  11. Faced with clear proof of a lie or wrongdoing, they…

    • Acknowledge the facts.
    • Are embarrassed, but end up admitting it.
    • Come up with a twisted excuse or minimise it.
    • Keep denying against the evidence, or turn the accusation back on me.

  12. When you have a success (work, a project, a compliment)…

    • They're proud of me and show it.
    • They're happy for me, even if not always demonstrative.
    • They play it down or find a flaw in my success.
    • They get jealous, sulk, or find a way to spoil the moment.

  13. When you bring up the idea of taking a step back or leaving…

    • They respect my need for space and we talk about it.
    • They're sad, but they hear me.
    • They guilt-trip me ('after everything I've done for you').
    • They threaten me: breakup as punishment, blackmail, 'I won't survive it', threats towards themselves or me.

  14. Do they use other people (an ex, colleagues, strangers) within your relationship?

    • No, never.
    • Rarely, and with no intent to hurt me.
    • They compare me to others or trigger my jealousy 'to test' me.
    • They accuse me of cheating for no reason, while keeping doubt or ambiguity alive themselves.

  15. Does your relationship feel like an emotional rollercoaster?

    • No, it's rather stable and reassuring.
    • There are ups and downs, like in any couple.
    • Yes: intense, all-consuming phases followed by cold breakups.
    • Yes, an exhausting cycle: they idealise me, tear me down, then come back the moment I pull away.

  16. When they promise to change after a crisis…

    • The change is real and it lasts.
    • They make an effort, with a few relapses.
    • It holds for a few days, then it all starts again.
    • Nothing ever changes: the promises only exist to make me stay.

  17. Since this relationship began, your self-confidence has…

    • Grown stronger.
    • Stayed stable.
    • Weakened: I often doubt myself.
    • Collapsed: I don't recognise myself anymore, I've lost who I was.

  18. In your relationship, the effort and the sacrifices…

    • Are balanced between the two of us.
    • Lean a little towards my side, but it stays fair.
    • Fall mainly on me (time, money, energy).
    • Are entirely one-way: I feel used with nothing in return.

  19. What do your loved ones say about your relationship?

    • They think it's healthy and are happy for me.
    • They don't have a particular opinion.
    • Some are worried or have already warned me.
    • Several have raised the alarm, but I downplay it or hide the reality from them.

  20. Today, the idea of leaving this relationship…

    • Isn't on the table: I feel good in it.
    • Crosses my mind sometimes, like in any couple.
    • Haunts me, but I feel unable to take the step.
    • Terrifies me: I know this relationship hurts me, but I feel 'addicted' and trapped.

Why do you feel the need to take this test?

Simply looking for a narcissistic partner test is rarely a coincidence. You don't wonder whether you're sharing your life with a manipulator when everything is fine. If you're here, a small voice has been whispering for a while that something is off, without you quite being able to name it.

The heart of a relationship with a narcissist isn't spectacular violence: it's constant doubt. “Am I too sensitive? Am I the problem? Am I overreacting?” This mental fog is no accident: it's exactly the effect manipulation is after, slowly eroding the trust you have in your own perception.

You're looking here for an outside, objective perspective, because from the inside everything blurs together. This test doesn't replace a professional's opinion, but it does separate what belongs to an ordinary toxic relationship from what belongs to genuine coercive control.

What exactly is a narcissist (or 'narcissistic pervert')?

The term narcissistic pervert (or covert narcissist), popularised by psychiatrist Marie-France Hirigoyen, describes a person who combines a narcissistic need for domination with a perverse way of relating: they feed on the other, draining their energy and self-esteem, without ever feeling genuine guilt.

Be careful, though, not to slap this label on too quickly. Having an ego, being selfish or going through a rough patch doesn't make someone a narcissist. The difference lies in intent and repetition:

  • •Someone simply clumsy or self-centred may hurt you without meaning to, but they're able to realise it, apologise sincerely and change their behaviour.
  • •A narcissist, on the other hand, instrumentalises: the relationship isn't a bond between equals, it's a power struggle in which your suffering counts for nothing against their need for control.

A narcissist doesn't first seek to be loved: they seek to control. And they arrange things so that you carry the blame for the harm they do to you.

The machinery of control: how you fall into the trap

If your score is high, a question may be eating at you: “How could I have let this happen?”. That guilt is unfair. You don't 'choose' to fall under a narcissist's control: you slide into it, through a frighteningly effective process.

Coercive control almost always sets in over three stages:

1. Love bombing (the ideal phase)

At first, it's a fairy tale. Constant attention, compliments, plans for the future, the feeling of having finally found your soulmate. This intense seduction creates a powerful attachment… and a wonderful memory you'll cling to later, when everything goes wrong.

2. Gradual devaluation

Once you're attached, the mask slips little by little. A criticism 'for your own good', a dig disguised as a joke, a humiliating comparison. Your threshold of tolerance quietly retreats, and you end up finding 'normal' what you'd never have accepted at the start.

3. Intermittent reinforcement (the addiction)

This is the most powerful trap. A narcissist isn't unpleasant 100% of the time: they alternate hot and cold, moments of fusion and icy breakups. These unpredictable returns of affection act like a drug on the brain and create genuine emotional dependency.

The signs that should really alert you

Beyond the score, it's certain specific mechanisms that signal narcissistic abuse. If you recognise several of them, your vigilance is justified. Many overlap, in fact, with the most destructive red flags in a relationship.

Gaslighting (denying your reality)

“I never said that”, “you're making it up”, “you're paranoid”. A narcissist denies facts you actually lived through, until you doubt your own memory and judgement. It's a silent form of psychological abuse that attacks your very identity.

Reversing the guilt

Whatever happens, it's always your fault. They systematically play the victim, turn your grievances back on you, and you end up apologising… for things you didn't do.

Isolation and control

Little by little, your friends 'don't deserve you', your family 'meddles in everything'. A narcissist cuts you off from your support network and monitors your time, your money or who you see, sometimes under the cover of love or jealousy.

The double face and the lack of empathy

Charming in public, contemptuous in private: “no one would believe me”. And faced with your pain, all you meet is a disconcerting coldness, sometimes irritation. This real lack of empathy is the signature of a narcissist.

The test suggests likely control: what now?

First, remember that this test is a tool for reflection, not a verdict. It never replaces a professional's opinion. If you think you're living in a controlling, even dangerous, relationship, surround yourself with truly competent people.

Becoming aware of the situation is the hardest step, and you've just taken it. A high score doesn't mean 'leave this minute' (except in cases of immediate physical danger), but it does mean the status quo is no longer sustainable. Here's where to start.

What's better to avoid

✗

Trying to 'save' or change them

You will never be your partner's therapist. Narcissistic perversion is a deep way of functioning that only the person concerned could decide to work on, which is exceedingly rare. Exhausting yourself trying to repair them will only harm you further.

✗

Confronting them with this test

Brandishing this result will almost certainly trigger a crisis, denial or a reversal ('you're the manipulator'). Keep it to yourself, as an inner compass, not as evidence to negotiate.

What's helpful to do

1

Document the facts

Keep a factual journal of the words and scenes that hurt you. Re-reading, in the cold light of day, what really happened is the best antidote to the doubt gaslighting plants in you.

2

Break the isolation

Talk to someone you trust and, ideally, to a professional (a psychologist) or an organisation specialised in psychological abuse. Saying things out loud strips away part of their power.

3

Set a limit and prepare your safety

Set a simple, firm limit on an unacceptable behaviour, then observe: a healthy partner adjusts, a narcissist escalates. There are things you should never accept, and you have every right to protect yourself.

The takeaway: your gut feeling is a compass

This narcissistic partner test isn't a court verdict: it's a mirror. If you answered these questions with a knot in your stomach, it's because your body already knows what your mind still struggles to admit.

A loving relationship should be a refuge, not a battlefield where you walk on eggshells. The confusion you feel isn't a weakness: it's the sign that you've been trying, for far too long, to adapt to an abnormal situation. You have the right to be respected, heard and safe, which is also what our healthy relationship test measures.

If this result worries you, don't carry this weight alone. Talking to someone is already a first step out of coercive control.
