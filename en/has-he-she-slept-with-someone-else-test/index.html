Has he or she slept with someone else?

15 questions to put order into what you have observed. We start by asking who we're talking about, because the questions aren't the same.

Ready for the test?

All 15 questions, in order. Start the test above to get your result.

  1. Has he been away from home more than before lately?

    • No, the schedule hasn't shifted at all.
    • A bit more, but he always tells me where he's going and with whom.
    • Yes, and the explanations stay vague when I ask.
    • Yes, and he gets annoyed the moment I ask where he was.

  2. How does he behave with his phone when you're next to him?

    • It lies around, I can pick it up and nobody minds.
    • It stays on him, but that has always been the case.
    • He turns it face down on the table, and he changed the passcode recently.
    • He gets aggressive if I come near, or he stays glued to me while I look.

  3. And your sex life, over the last few weeks?

    • The same as usual, no more and no less.
    • A little less often, but we're going through a busy stretch.
    • It stopped almost overnight, with no explanation.
    • It changed in nature: new habits that came out of nowhere.

  4. Has he changed his appearance in the last few months?

    • No, nothing in particular.
    • He got back into sport, we'd talked about it together.
    • New perfume, new clothes, new haircut: all of it at once.
    • He mostly gets ready on the days he goes out without me.

  5. Does one name keep coming up in what he tells you?

    • No, he talks about the same people as before.
    • Yes, a new colleague or a new friend, mentioned naturally.
    • One name came up constantly, then he stopped mentioning it overnight.
    • He plays that person down the moment I ask anything about them.

  6. What's going on with the social media side?

    • Nothing new, I see pretty much everything.
    • He spends more time on it than before, but hides nothing.
    • Notifications are muted, or I saw a conversation archived.
    • I saw a message go by that he deleted right afterwards.

  7. Does he call you jealous or paranoid when you ask a question?

    • No, he answers plainly.
    • He sighs a bit, but ends up answering.
    • Yes, and the conversation always ends up turned against me.
    • Yes, and he accuses me of cheating when I'm not.

  8. Have you noticed spending you can't account for?

    • No, the accounts are clear between us.
    • A few cash withdrawals, nothing unusual for him.
    • Restaurants, hotels or gifts I've never heard a word about.
    • He opened an account or a card he never mentioned.

  9. Do the explanations hold up when you cross-check them?

    • Yes, everything lines up.
    • He gets a detail wrong now and then, like anyone.
    • One or two versions didn't match, and he corrected them afterwards.
    • I have proof he lied to me about where he was.

  10. How do the people close to him behave around you?

    • The same as before, no awkwardness.
    • I see them less, but our schedules changed.
    • There's a pause when I walk in, or subjects we avoid in front of me.
    • Someone warned me, or said something half-spoken.

  11. Has he become attentive in a way that isn't like him?

    • He's the way he has always been.
    • He's making an effort, and we'd talked about it beforehand.
    • He buys me things for no reason, which isn't his style.
    • He swings with no warning: very present, then cold and distant.

  12. What happens when you offer to come along somewhere?

    • It makes them happy, it has never been an issue.
    • Sometimes he'd rather go alone, and he says so plainly.
    • He always finds a good reason for me not to come.
    • He has already cancelled an outing because I wanted to come.

  13. Do they ever come home very late, or not at all?

    • No, never.
    • Yes, but it's planned in advance and I know who he's with.
    • More and more often, and I find out at the last minute.
    • Yes, and he showers or changes clothes before even talking to me.

  14. Has he brought up condoms again when you'd stopped using them?

    • No, nothing has changed on that front.
    • We talked it over together, for a reason we decided as a couple.
    • He pushed to start again, without really explaining why.
    • Yes, and he cut the conversation short when I asked why.

  15. And your gut feeling, honestly, what does it say?

    • Nothing much, I'm taking this test out of curiosity.
    • I have a doubt, but mostly I think I'm imagining things.
    • Something has changed, I've felt it for weeks.
    • I'm almost certain, I'm just missing the proof.
How does this test work?
  1. You say whether your partner is a man or a woman. The fifteen questions adapt, and two of them change completely.
  2. Answer with the last few weeks in mind, not your whole history. A sign counts because it changed, not because it exists.
  3. Each question carries a weight matching how serious it is: an unexplained expense counts for more than a new haircut. The total, from 0 to 155 points, places you in one of four reading levels.
  4. The result tells you what your observations weigh together, and what it is reasonable to do with them.

Answer honestly, including when the honest answer is "I don't know". A reassuring result obtained by rounding off the corners is worth nothing.

The question that keeps going round

You may not have told anyone. It comes back in the evening, in the car, when the conversation dies down. Have they slept with someone else? And right behind it, a second question, often more painful: am I losing my mind, or am I right?

Doubt has this peculiarity that it feeds itself. A late arrival becomes a lie, a phone turned face down becomes proof, a silence becomes a confession. Then the next day everything looks normal and you blame yourself for having thought it. That back and forth is exhausting, and it damages the relationship just as surely as what you fear.

This test will not decide for you, and it would be dishonest to pretend otherwise. What it does is line up what you have actually observed, separate the signals that carry weight from the ones that carry none, and give you a whole picture instead of fifteen details spinning around in your head.

The signs that count, and the ones that prove nothing

None of these signs is worth anything on its own. What counts is the recent change, and the fact that several show up at the same time.

The phone that changes status

The signal isn't "they protect their phone", it's "they have been protecting it since recently". New passcode, screen turned over, notifications muted, a conversation archived. Someone who has always been private tells you nothing at all.

Versions that don't hold

This is the heaviest signal of all, because it can be checked. An evening told twice with two different places, an explanation corrected afterwards. A lie about the place weighs more than a lie about the time.

Intimacy that tips over

In both directions. A sudden stop is a warning, but so is a change of nature: new habits that come out of nowhere raise as many questions as a sudden desert.

The accusation turned around

You ask a question, and you end up justifying yourself. When someone accuses their partner of cheating with nothing to suggest it, therapists call that projection. It isn't proof, but it is worth noting.

Money leaves traces

A double life is expensive. Unusual cash withdrawals, restaurant or hotel spending invisible in the shared budget, an account opened without a word. Bank statements lie less well than people.

The circle that turns awkward

A pause when you walk in, a friend who avoids you, a half-spoken sentence. Those close by often know first, and they cope badly with knowing. Their discomfort is an indirect signal, and it shows.

Two signs are almost never enough. The word investigators use is "cluster": it is the convergence of several clues over the same period that changes the reading, not their raw number.

What your score says, and what it doesn't

Each question carries a weight matching how serious it is, and each answer a value matching its rank. The total runs from 0 to 155 points. The four levels below are not verdicts, they are ways of reading what you answered. A high score describes a behaviour, never a fact.

ScoreLevelWhat it means
0 to 11Nothing pointing that wayNone of the usual signals. If the doubt persists, it probably comes from somewhere other than their behaviour.
12 to 23Ambiguous signsChange, yes. But stress, exhaustion and a phase of withdrawal produce exactly the same symptoms.
24 to 35A real clusterToo many things converge for it to be chance. Careful: depression or a hidden problem gives the same profile.
36 to 45A lot of signalsSecrecy, verified inconsistencies, withdrawal of intimacy. It still proves nothing, but it calls for an answer.

A score of 40 is not proof, and a score of 8 is not a guarantee. Both mainly tell you what your observation is worth today, with the elements you have.

Sleeping elsewhere and getting attached elsewhere are not the same question

Many people arrive here with a precise question: was there a physical act? That is a different question from "is my relationship in danger", and it calls for different signs. Physical infidelity leaves material traces: absences, spending, inconsistencies about places, sometimes a change in intimacy.

Emotional infidelity leaves almost nothing material. It shows in attention that moves elsewhere, in confidences that go somewhere else, in a new closeness with someone who is never mentioned in front of you. It can hurt just as much, and last longer, without a single night spent away.

This test focuses on the first question. If your doubt is more about the second, infidelity is measured differently: it covers the emotional kind as much as the physical one. And if what is eating at you is mostly jealousy, with nothing concrete behind it, the jealousy test fits better.

What you should not do right now

Doubt pushes you to act, and almost every action it inspires makes things worse. Here are the three most common ones.

Going through the phone

It's the first idea, and it's the worst. At best you find nothing, and the doubt stays intact because "they must have deleted it". At worst you find something ambiguous that you will never be able to forget or verify. And in both cases, you now have something to hide too.

Investigating through their friends

Questioning their friends puts everyone in an impossible position and always gets back to them, usually distorted. You lose the element of surprise, you gain a reputation, and you get no answer.

Accusing without laying anything out

"I know you're cheating on me" never produces a confession. It produces a counter-attack, and it puts you in the position of having to prove something. If you accuse with nothing to show, you have lost the discussion before starting it.

If you are tempted by spyware or a tracking device, know that installing that on someone's phone without their consent is a criminal offence in most countries. Beyond the legal risk, it is exactly the kind of act that will cost you the benefit of the truth if it ever comes out.

How to ask the question, in practice

There is no magic wording, but there are conditions that clearly raise your chances of getting a real answer rather than a defensive reflex.

Choose the moment, not the impulse

Not at midnight after a night out, not as one of you is leaving for work, not in front of the children. A moment when nobody has an escape route or an appointment in twenty minutes.

Describe, don't label

"You came home at three on Saturday and told me you were at Tom's, and Tom told me the opposite" is hard to brush aside. "You lie to me all the time" gets brushed aside in one sentence. Stay on the facts you actually observed, without inflating them.

Say what you're looking for

Announcing "I'd rather know, even if it hurts, than carry on like this" often changes the conversation. Many people lie not out of a taste for lying but out of fear of the reaction. Removing part of that fear can be enough.

Listen to what isn't said too

An answer that takes ten seconds to arrive is not proof. An answer that changes version between Tuesday and Thursday is. What you are looking for isn't a reaction, it's consistency over time.

And if the answer is yes?

Then you enter another question, and you don't have to answer it that same evening. The first thing to know is that the decision to stay or leave is not taken in the days that follow the revelation. Shock is a poor adviser, in either direction.

What determines the rest comes down to very little: does the person take responsibility without minimising, do they cut contact, do they accept your questions for months without tiring of them. Without those three, rebuilding rarely holds. With them, it is possible, and it generally takes between one and three years.

And if you want to take stock afterwards of what is left, the healthy relationship test and the trust test talk about what comes next rather than about the shock.

If the situation puts you in danger, if you are afraid of your partner's reaction when you ask a question, this is no longer a question of infidelity. A domestic abuse helpline in your country can take that call, free and confidentially.

FAQ

Can a test really tell whether someone slept with another person?
No, and no serious test will claim it can. What this test measures is the consistency and the density of what you have observed. It turns fifteen scattered impressions into one overall reading. Certainty never comes from a questionnaire: it comes from a conversation, an admission, or something concrete.
Why ask whether my partner is a man or a woman?
Because one of the fifteen questions only makes sense in one of the two cases, and because a question written for a man reads badly when you're thinking of a woman. The choice changes neither the scoring nor the result levels: the other fourteen questions are identical, only the wording follows.
My score is high, does that mean they cheated on me?
No. A high score means a lot of signals piled up over the same period. An undisclosed depression, a hidden dismissal, a debt, a health problem kept secret or an addiction produce exactly the same answer profile. That is why the result points you towards a conversation, not towards a conclusion.
My score is low but I still have a doubt, is that normal?
Yes, and it's common. Doubt doesn't always come from the other person. An old betrayal, in this relationship or a previous one, leaves a vigilance that wakes up without a present-day reason. If the doubt persists while nothing feeds it, that is the side to look at, and talking about it remains the best way out.
Can I take this test with my partner?
It isn't designed for that, and it would be a bad idea: the fifteen questions describe suspicions, and having someone you suspect read them amounts to accusing them one question at a time. Take it alone, then talk, in your own words.
Are my answers stored anywhere?
No. Everything happens in your browser, no answer is sent or kept, and there is no account and no email address to give. Close the page and nothing remains.
