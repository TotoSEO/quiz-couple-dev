One couple in three argues on the drive to their holiday, and it started before they left

Relationship
Published on August 12, 2026 | Thomas | 9 min read |

Quick summary

  • 28% of people in a relationship find the journey to their holiday tense, and in 83% of trips a single person drives the whole way.
  • 47% of women and 38% of men have already argued about organising the holiday, before leaving at all.
  • The number one trigger is not the destination: it is the imbalance in the planning, cited by 40% of women.
  • Once there, 49% think sharing a space increases arguments… while 73% rate themselves as very easy to travel with.
  • Going over budget comes next, affecting 30% of couples.
  • What helps most is not better communication but scheduled time apart: around two hours a day.

Let us start with the number, since that is what made you click: 28% of people in a relationship say the drive to their holiday is a tense moment, according to an OpinionWay survey run for Direct Assurance among 1,016 people in May 2025. The press rounded it up to "one couple in three", and that is not unfair.

Except that figure describes the car. It does not describe the start of the story. Because another survey, also French, shows that the argument very often began several weeks earlier, in the planning: 47% of women in a relationship and 38% of men say they have already argued with their partner about organising the holiday.

So no, do not cancel the trip! But there is real value in knowing where it breaks, because it is almost never where you think.

Why holidays surface what was already dormant

Couples do not argue because they are going on holiday. They argue because a holiday removes, all at once, the three things that were hiding the problem for the rest of the year: work, mismatched schedules and separate rooms.

For eleven months, two people who do not really agree on how things get organised can live together perfectly well: each handles their own day, you cross paths in the evening, there is no time to discuss it. On holiday that buffer disappears. You are together sixteen hours a day, in a smaller space than home, with decisions to make every two hours.

That is why holidays do not create the problems: they make them visible, very fast and all at the same time.

The 6 figures, in the order the argument arrives

The chronology matters more than the percentages taken separately. Here is how it unfolds, from May through to the second week away.

  1. 47% of women, 38% of men: the share of people who have already argued with their partner about organising the holiday. It is the very first friction point, and it lands before the booking.

  2. 66% of women say they do more than their partner in the preparation, including 43% who say "a lot more". Facing them, 53% of men believe they took an equal share, against only 27% of women. That is not a disagreement about the holiday, it is a disagreement about what happened.

  3. 30%: the share of couples who argue about going over budget. A stable figure, and the only one a spreadsheet genuinely solves.

  4. 28% find the journey tense. Route choice and driving style top the list of reasons, far ahead of tiredness or the children.

  5. 83%: the share of trips where one person drives from start to finish, usually a man. Roughly one woman in three says she would rather not take the wheel for fear of her partner's comments.

  6. 49%: once there, nearly one person in two thinks sharing a space with their travel companions increases the likelihood of arguments.

📌 Worth knowing

None of these figures measures the same thing, and that is precisely the point. We are not talking about a couple arguing once: we are talking about four distinct moments, in May, in June, on the motorway and after three days in a rental. A couple can clear the first three without trouble and blow up on the fourth.

The trigger is almost never the destination

This is the most common misreading, and it is an expensive one: as long as you look for the problem in the choice of hotel, you will not find it.

The destination only comes fourth

People assume they argue because one wanted the sea and the other the mountains… but in the IFOP survey, disagreement over the destination concerns only 26% of respondents, men and women combined. It is real, but it comes a long way down the list.

The number one reason, given by 40% of women, is the partner's lack of involvement. Not "you picked the wrong place", but "I did all of it on my own".

One very visible task against ten nobody sees

When you look at the task breakdown, you see why it eventually surfaces. On family holidays, women mostly report starting the planning (56% against 31%), booking the accommodation (48% against 26%), cooking once there (54% against 24%) and packing the children's bags, at close to 80% against roughly 10%. The men drive: 58% against 18%.

Driving is visible. Having remembered the medical records and the spare shoes is not. That is exactly the ground on which a disagreement that never gets said out loud ends up erupting over something tiny, usually the toll booth or the sat nav.

The paradox that explains all the rest

Here is the funniest statistic in the whole file, and probably the most useful.

In a survey conducted in March 2026 by Talker Research among 2,000 Americans who travel with loved ones, 73% of respondents consider themselves the perfect travel companion. Easy-going, accommodating, never a problem.

In the same survey, 49% think sharing a space increases arguments.

Both figures cannot be true at once. If nearly three people in four are perfect, where do the arguments come from? The authors call it the vacation compatibility gap: everyone measures their own flexibility by their intentions, and the other person's by their behaviour.

👍 The real triggers

  • The planning imbalance, named by 40% of women as the leading reason.
  • The budget overrunning, 30% of couples.
  • The lack of personal space, once you are there.
  • Pace: one wants five sights a day, the other wants to sleep.

👎 What gets blamed unfairly

  • The destination, involved for only 26% of respondents.
  • The weather, which mostly serves as a pretext for tension already present.
  • The country chosen: no serious survey shows one destination causes more arguments than another.
  • Going away at all: the problem is not the trip, it is what the trip reveals.

What defuses it, and it is not "communicate better"

The usual answer to this kind of article is "talk to each other". Except the figures point somewhere else, and it is considerably easier to apply.

Two hours a day, each on your own

In the Talker Research survey, 77% of respondents say having personal space eases tension, and 68% say time alone makes them feel more connected to their travel group. The median need sits around two hours a day.

Two hours is not a separate bedroom, and it is not a separate holiday: it is a coffee while the other one naps. And it goes in the plan beforehand, otherwise nobody ever takes it.

Novelty rather than comfort

A study published in 2024 in Annals of Tourism Research Empirical Insights followed 238 people in relationships, then 102 couples actually travelling together. The researchers measured how many novel, interesting or slightly challenging experiences happened during the trip.

The result: the more there were, the higher the passion, relationship satisfaction and physical intimacy after coming home. And the effect did not depend on how long the couple had been together, from one year to over thirty.

Two written lists, not a "help me"

"Help me" leaves the allocating to the person already carrying it: they still have to decide what to hand over, explain it, then check it got done. Two separate written lists, with a name against every line, delete that invisible step.

And for the journey itself, the fix is almost silly: give the passenger something to do. When tension rises in the car, 40% of couples go quiet, which settles nothing and installs two hours of frost until the next service station.

With children, the equation changes

This is the part where most articles invent a figure. I am going to do the opposite.

What no serious survey lets anyone claim

I found no reliable data allowing anyone to write "X% of couples with children argue against Y% without". The figures circulating on this come from commercial polls with no published methodology, and I would rather leave the cell empty.

Same for destinations: nothing shows that one country causes more arguments than another. If you read that somewhere, ask for the sample size!

What is documented: the load shifts

Packing the children's bags, the first aid kit, choosing suitable clothes and picking the activities fall to women in 75 to 86% of cases depending on the task. That is not an argument in itself, it is the reservoir the argument draws from.

On accommodation, the need changes nature too: 70% of parents travelling with their children consider several bedrooms essential, against 58% of travellers overall. That is not luxury, it is the only way to recover the two hours mentioned above.

If the subject comes back identical every year, the holiday is not the problem: it is how the parenting load gets split the rest of the year, which simply becomes impossible to ignore once four of you are shut into forty square metres.

What couples ask before they book

Is arguing on holiday a bad sign?
No, not in itself. What counts is the subject and the ending. An argument about the sat nav that stops at the service station has nothing to do with an argument about involvement that has come back every summer for six years. The second one is about the rest of the year.

Should we go away separately?
It is not a silly idea, and it is not an admission of failure. But before getting there, the cheapest option is to keep the same trip and build time apart into it. The difference between two people suffocating and two people doing fine often comes down to two hours a day.

How do we know whether we actually want the same holiday?
By answering separately before discussing it, rather than negotiating out loud. Comparing written answers defuses a lot: it stops being you against them and becomes the two of you facing your actual common ground, measured instead of assumed.

What if the holiday really ended badly?
One difficult week does not say much. One difficult week that looks like the previous eleven months does. In that case the useful question is not "why did we argue in Corsica", but what makes a relationship liveable day to day.

Where do we start before the next trip?
With a conversation that is not about logistics. It sounds backwards, but most July tension is prepared in March, when nobody is saying anything. Asking what the other one wants from the trip avoids discovering on arrival that one wanted to rest and the other to see everything.

Three things to settle before the boot closes

There is no couple that never argues on holiday, and there is no magic destination. What there is, is a fairly predictable sequence: unbalanced planning, a journey where one person makes every call, and accommodation where nobody has a spot of their own.

The good news is that all three are fixable before leaving, at a kitchen table, in twenty minutes. What cannot be fixed on site is the grievance you have been carrying since March. That one has to come out before the boot closes.

📚 Sources cited

OpinionWay for Direct Assurance, 1,016 people aged 18 and over, 6 and 7 May 2025. IFOP for Voyage avec Nous, 1,099 people in relationships, 22 to 24 June 2022. Talker Research for Club Wyndham, 2,000 Americans who travel with loved ones, 5 to 11 March 2026. Academic research by Coffey, Shahvali, Kerstetter and Aron, Annals of Tourism Research Empirical Insights, 2024.
